As a landlord in Texas, protecting yourself from liability is essential to running a successful rental business. From creating a strong lease agreement to ensuring compliance with housing laws, taking the right precautions can help you avoid legal pitfalls and costly disputes.

This article outlines key steps to reduce your risk, including proper documentation, routine maintenance, and clear communication with tenants. By proactively addressing potential issues, you can safeguard your property, maintain positive tenant relationships, and limit your exposure to liability.

*Key Takeaways*

To minimize liability in Texas, it’s worth using the Texas Association of Realtors (TAR) Lease Agreement, which is widely recognized and regularly vetted by attorneys and industry experts. This lease is comprehensive and designed to meet state requirements, covering key areas such as rent, maintenance, and tenant responsibilities.

Using this standardized lease reduces the risk of missing important legal protections. While some unique tailoring may be needed for specific situations, creating your own lease or using an unfamiliar template can lead to legal complications. The TAR lease provides a solid foundation to minimize liability and protect your interests.

Regular property maintenance reduces liability by allowing landlords to catch issues early, ensuring the property is well-maintained and the tenant is properly caring for it. Routine inspections help identify potential problems, such as malfunctioning appliances or structural concerns, before they lead to costly repairs or safety hazards.

For example, regularly draining a water heater can extend its lifespan and prevent a sudden failure that could cause flooding and extensive damage, potentially costing tens of thousands of dollars in repairs. Stay proactive with maintenance so as to avoid larger issues, minimize repair costs, and reduce the risk of liability from property damage or tenant injury.

To avoid discrimination claims under Texas and federal law, it’s important to establish clear, objective qualifications for tenants and stick to them. These qualifications might include income requirements, credit history, or no prior evictions. Avoid considering any factors unrelated to these qualifications, such as race, gender, ethnicity, or other protected characteristics.

If you happen to learn information about a tenant applicant that relates to protected categories, ensure that your decision is based solely on the predetermined qualifications. This consistent, objective approach helps you comply with fair housing laws and avoid potential discrimination claims.
