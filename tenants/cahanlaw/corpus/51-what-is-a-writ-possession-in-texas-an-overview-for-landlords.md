A **writ of possession** and a **notice to vacate** are two key steps in the eviction process, but they serve different purposes.

**A writ of possession** is the final court order requiring a tenant to leave the property. It is issued after the landlord wins a judgment and no appeal is filed. The landlord must request the writ, which the court then signs.

Once signed, the writ directs the constable to post a 24-hour notice on the property. While the notice gives the tenant a short window to vacate, enforcement involves the constable coordinating with the landlord for a final move-out which can be a few days to a few weeks.

At that point, the landlord must have a team ready to enter the property, address any locked doors, and remove the tenant’s belongings. After the locks are changed, the landlord takes control of everything inside. Items left outside are accessible to the tenant for 24 hours before being considered abandoned.

**A notice to vacate**, on the other hand, is the *first* step in an eviction. It notifies the tenant they must leave the property within a specific number of days. The landlord isn’t required to explain why unless the tenant is on housing assitance.

This notice is a necessary step before filing an eviction case in court, while the writ of possession is the final step in enforcing the tenant’s removal.

Determining when to file for a writ of possession in Travis County depends on specific circumstances following a judgment and the tenant’s status at the property.

Once the judgment is final and no appeal has been filed, the first step is to assess whether the tenant is still in possession of the property. If they are, you’ll need to file for a writ of possession.

If you believe the tenant has vacated, verify this by inspecting the property. Knock on the door and confirm whether anyone is still present. Check inside, if possible, to determine if any personal property remains.

Ideally, the tenant should explicitly state they have vacated. A clear statement like, “I and all other occupants have vacated the property, hereby return possession of the property, and abandon any personal property that may have been left behind,” removes the need for a writ of possession.

Once a writ of possession is issued, there is a brief period between its issuance, the constable posting it, and the scheduled move-out. During this time, the tenant is still legally allowed to remain on the property. The landlord cannot take action to remove the tenant until the constable enforces the writ.

When the constable arrives to enforce the writ, they will enter the property, remove any occupants, and ensure the tenant vacates. If the tenant cooperates, the constable may allow them to gather their belongings, with any additional time granted at the landlord’s discretion.

However, if the tenant refuses to leave or ignores the constable’s orders, they can face arrest.

Once the property is cleared, tenants have 24 hours to retrieve any belongings left outside. If they attempt to re-enter the house during this period, it is considered trespassing or breaking and entering, and the landlord can call law enforcement.

If a former tenant is found on or near the property after the 24-hour window, landlords should notify the police. Inform law enforcement that a writ of possession was executed, so the individual is no longer permitted on the property. The police may then take action, potentially charging the person with criminal trespass and making an arrest.
